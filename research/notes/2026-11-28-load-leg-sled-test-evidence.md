# Load legs on rear-facing infant seats: sled-test evidence

## Question

The Aria V2 base has a load leg; the Mesa V3 and KeyFit 30 bases do not.
Does published testing show a load leg reduces injury measures? Raised by
the devil's-advocate review of the Aria V2 choice, 2026-11-28.

## Sources

- [Patton DA, Belwadi AN, Maheshwari J, Arbogast KB, "Rearward-Facing
  Infant Child Restraint Systems with Support Legs in Frontal and
  Frontal-Oblique Impacts", *International Journal of Environmental
  Research and Public Health*, 2021-10-14, doi 10.3390/ijerph182010799](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8535498/)
  — independent testing (peer-reviewed sled study, Children's Hospital of
  Philadelphia authors). Abstract read in full with Firecrawl,
  2026-11-28; citation details from the page metadata.

## Findings

- **Method:** frontal and frontal-oblique sled tests "using the simulated
  Consumer Reports test method", with and without a plate representing a
  front seatback. A Q1.5 crash dummy in rear-facing infant seats, "tested
  with and without support legs".
- **Result:** "The presence of a support leg was associated with
  significant reductions of head injury metrics below injury tolerance
  limits for all tests, which supports the findings of previous studies."
- "The presence of a support leg was also associated with significant
  reductions of peak neck tensile force."
- The authors' limit: how well the seatback plate represents a real car
  "is not well understood".
- The abstract does not name the seat models tested. Whether the Aria V2
  was among them is not stated.

## Confidence

**Medium.** A peer-reviewed lab study, read at its source (abstract only).
It shows load legs help in general. It does not test the Aria V2, and sled
tests are not real crashes.
